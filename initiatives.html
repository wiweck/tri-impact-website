Living Art Traditions
  • Livelihood Security
  • Decent Work

Living Art Traditions brings skills, provenance, buyers, savings and health together around each artist.

India's art traditions are admired worldwide, yet the artists behind them often stay poor, unseen and unprotected. Living Art Traditions works with artists of Madhubani, Gond, Sanjhi, miniature, Zardozi and other traditions, and runs five supports together, around the same artists.

Natural Colours
  • Climate Action
  • Livelihood Security

An invasive plant that adds fuel to forest fires, turned into natural dye and income for artisans.

Lantana camara spreads through the forest and farmland of the Garhwal Himalayas, crowds out native vegetation and raises the fuel load that drives forest fires. Natural Colours trains artisan communities, largely women, to extract dye from it, and from native plants and food waste. A handbook, three learning modules and a catalogue of more than 80 dye-yielding plants sit in an open repository anyone can use.

Partner platformVeraAnchaloffers botanical colour materials to textile buyers

Sources
  • Lantana and other invasive plants
  • Native plants: walnut, wild indigo, rhododendron
  • Food waste: pomegranate rind, onion skin, fruit peel
Methods
  • Boiling, for roots, bark and leaves
  • Fermentation, for indigo
  • Solar or cold dyeing, for flowers and fine fibres
What it builds
  • Natural dye artisans can use and sell
  • An open repository of more than 80 plants
  • Bilingual handbook and three modules
Climate action and adaptation

  • Puts an invasive plant to work

    Lantana that adds fuel to forest fires becomes a raw material, so clearing it earns an income.

  • Harvests within limits

    Never more than 20 to 30 percent of the plant material at one site, for native and invasive species alike.

  • Replaces synthetic inputs

    Dye from plants and food waste in place of synthetic dye, made by the artisans who use it.

Dung to Dignity
  • Livelihood Security
  • Climate Action

Livestock dung, turned into compost for the family farm and for sale.

Farmers in Uttarakhand already use dung and crop residues, and some sell compost. What they lack is a complete support system: a clear picture of the material a village has, safe processing, quality checks, good use on the farm and sales at a fair margin. Dung to Dignity builds that system with village households. The first learning site is Gaindi Khata in Haridwar district.

It grows from Tri-Impact's Climate Success Mapping work, which documented composting and vermicomposting initiatives in Haridwar and Dehradun.

Two pathways, measured separately

Start

Livestock dung and farm biomass

Process

Safe composting and quality checks

Pathway 1

Farm use

Compost goes onto the household's own fields.

Measured: soil and crop response, fertiliser spending, farm income.

Pathway 2

Market sales

Producers sell consistent compost to farmers and nurseries.

Measured: production cost, price, repeat demand, net income.

How it works

Each cycleshapes the next
  1. 01Map the village
  2. 02Train and equip
  3. 03Produce and check
  4. 04Use and sell
  5. 05Review and adapt
  1. 01

    Map the village

    Available dung and biomass, current practice, space, water, farm needs and buyers.

  2. 02

    Train and equip

    Safe handling, pre-composting, and vermicomposting in individual or shared beds.

  3. 03

    Produce and check

    Each batch recorded for materials, labour, output and quality, such as moisture, pH and maturity.

  4. 04

    Use and sell

    Compost goes onto participating farms and into buyer trials.

  5. 05

    Review and adapt

    Results from each cycle shape the next, as the SEED-SCALE method sets out.

Climate adaptation

  • Soil that holds water

    Organic matter improves soil structure and helps it take in and hold water, which matters on rainfed hill farms.

  • Less purchased fertiliser

    Compost can replace part of the chemical fertiliser a farm buys. The initiative measures how much.

  • Cleaner villages

    Dung that is collected, stored and processed well leaves surroundings cleaner and safer.

Each learning site produces a costed package another team can use in another village: site-selection criteria, a resource and market-mapping tool, technical guidance, record formats and unit costs.

From Organic Resources to Green Enterprises
  • Climate Action
  • Livelihood Security

Lantana, pine needles and crop residues, turned into biochar and rural enterprises.

Biochar is a stable, charcoal-like material made by heating biomass with little oxygen. It is used to improve farm and nursery soils, to restore degraded land and to help remediate contaminated soil. From Organic Resources to Green Enterprises builds rural enterprises in Uttarakhand that turn local biomass into quality-tested biochar for these uses.

Biomass, with safeguards

  • Lantana, an invasive plant

    Only from authorised removal, with native plant recovery under the responsible authority's plan.

  • Pine needles

    From designated collection areas, within limits that protect the forest floor.

  • Crop residues

    Surplus maize stalks and pulse residues, after fodder, mulch and fuel needs are met.

Biochar

Made by qualified operators with established equipment at shared or partner facilities, and tested batch by batch against its intended use.

Where it is used
  • Farm and orchard soils
  • Nurseries and horticulture
  • Restoring degraded land
  • Soil remediation

How each enterprise is built

  1. 01

    Secure the biomass

    Recoverable supply, permissions and cost per tonne, before anything is built.

  2. 02

    Produce safely

    Trained operators, checks on smoke, fire and dust, and safe cooling and storage.

  3. 03

    Prove the quality

    Batch records and testing against the specification each buyer needs.

  4. 04

    Win repeat buyers

    Trials, delivered prices and repeat orders before production grows.

  5. 05

    Show it on the farm

    Customer farms track crop and soil response alongside the cost of use.

  6. 06

    Stand on its own

    Clear ownership, fair pay for the people who gather biomass, and women's and young people's earnings tracked.

Climate action and adaptation

  • Carbon held in soil

    Biomass that would otherwise burn or rot becomes a stable form of carbon applied to soil. The initiative measures net carbon removal rather than assuming it.

  • Farms that cope with dry spells

    Customer farm trials measure how biochar affects soil moisture and crop response on farms facing erratic rainfall.

  • Invasive plant cleared responsibly

    Lantana comes only from authorised removal, and native plant recovery follows the responsible authority's plan.

Every enterprise is planned to earn from product sales first. The initiative follows SEED-SCALE: it learns from successful biomass enterprises in Uttarakhand and northern India, and hands defined roles to capable local actors over time.
