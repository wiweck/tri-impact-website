Living Art Traditions

What it takes for an art tradition to pay: skills, savings, health and a route to buyers.

Living Art Traditions works with artists and artisans to improve how they work and what they earn. Training, financial literacy and pathways to market strengthen livelihoods, and health and eye-care support decent work.

The gap

India's art traditions are admired worldwide. The artists behind them often stay poor, unseen and unprotected.

The skill is not in question. What lets artists down is everything around the work: who sets the price, who gets the credit, what the work costs the body, and who can reach a bank, a scheme or a buyer. India has no national survey of traditional artists, so the evidence comes from studies of single art forms. The same problems appear in one after another.

  1. 01

    Artists rarely set their own price

    Middlemen place the orders, choose the designs and keep most of the margin, and few artists can reach buyers on their own.

  2. 02

    The designs travel without the artist

    Motifs are copied onto mass-market and luxury goods, and the artist's name rarely goes with the work. Copies compete on price, and the credit goes elsewhere.

  3. 03

    Fine work costs the body

    Hours of close, precise work bring pain and eye strain. Much of it is done at home, often at night, without dry storage or health cover.

  4. 04

    Savings, credit and schemes stay out of reach

    Income comes order by order, savings are thin, and few artists know which government schemes they qualify for.

  5. 05

    Gender, caste and distance limit access

    Women do much of the work, yet in several traditions men handle the trade and the price. In some places caste has shaped who could paint what and who met buyers, and distance keeps many artists waiting for buyers to come to them.

  6. 06

    The materials, walls and next generation are thinning

    Natural colours are giving way to synthetic paint, the mud walls and rituals some traditions grew from are disappearing, and fewer young people take up the work.

What has already worked

Programmes in several traditions show that these problems respond to the right support.

Read more about each approach →

  • Training

    About 1,250 Warli women learned to apply their art to new materials, formed producer groups and took paid orders. Four of six groups opened bank accounts.

  • Co-creation

    A Sanjhi paper-cut master from Mathura worked with a contemporary artist, and the work was shown at Homo Faber 2026 in Venice.

  • Direct and fair sales

    157 Gond artists sell online under a written agreement that gives them 60% of profits. Artists near Ranthambhore who stopped selling through Jaipur dealers found ready buyers.

  • Making artists visible

    Sohrai and Khovar artists learned to paint on paper, and a register of nearly 500 artists helps buyers find them. Their work covers a 17,500 sq ft public wall in Ranchi.

  • Community-held protection

    A Warli-led organisation holds the Warli Geographical Indication, so the right to the name rests with the community.

Traditions and expertise

A Padma Shri, national awards and six generations of practice.

Living Art Traditions works with master artists and the families that keep these forms alive, from miniature painting and Sanjhi paper-cut to Sohrai, Khovar, Saura and Zardozi. Their work has been honoured by the Government of India and shown in Venice, London, New York and Hong Kong.

  • Padma Shri 2013

    Syed Shakir Ali

    Miniature · Jaipur, Rajasthan

    A master of Mughal and Company School painting, he has restored historic paintings at Jaipur's City Palace and exhibited in Belgium, Mauritius, Algeria and Hong Kong.

  • Homo Faber 2026, Venice

    Mohan Kumar Verma

    Sanjhi · Mathura, Uttar Pradesh

    A national award winner with three decades of practice. His paper sculptures with artist Sumakshi Singh took Sanjhi from temple stencils to Venice.

  • UNESCO Award 2012

    Ram Soni

    Sanjhi · Alwar, Rajasthan

    Cuts lace-like designs freehand, often without a sketch. National Award 2002 and Shilp Shiromani 2008, with exhibitions in Singapore, London, Argentina and Switzerland.

  • National Award 2018

    Praveen Kumar Saini

    Miniature · Jaipur, Rajasthan

    A third-generation artist who paints birds and flowers with lapis lazuli and malachite on wasli, silk and canvas, with solo shows in Mauritius, London and New York.

  • Trains other women

    Rudhan Devi

    Sohrai and Khovar · Hazaribagh, Jharkhand

    After losing her right hand, she kept painting with her left. Her work was shown in Emami Art's Convergences exhibition in 2026.

  • Third generation

    Mohammad Bilal

    Zardozi · Agra, Uttar Pradesh

    Son of a Padma Shri awardee, he holds a master's in fine arts, won the Excellence in Zardozi Award in 2025 and joins Kalamkari with Zardozi in shared works.

  • Sixth generation

    Ashutosh Verma

    Sanjhi · Mathura, Uttar Pradesh

    A Roshan Kalapesi Award winner whose work was shown in Sanjhi Revisited at the India Habitat Centre, New Delhi. He teaches workshops to pass the form on.

  • Fifth generation

    Mohan Prajapati

    Miniature · Jaipur, Rajasthan

    Works across the Mughal, Kishangarh, Jaipur, Kangra and Jain styles, sometimes with a single-hair brush, in detail that shows fully only under magnification.

  • A 3 mm paper boat

    Sakshi Garg

    Sanjhi · Bahadurgarh, Haryana

    Known for paper-cut work at the smallest scale, she holds the Haryana State Handicraft Award and has exhibited in New Delhi.

  • State Award 2017

    Love Kumar Soni

    Sanjhi · Mathura, Uttar Pradesh

    Turns handmade paper into detailed birds, nature and narrative scenes, and teaches the form to students.

  • Documented by Lalit Kala Akademi

    Rabinath Sabar

    Saura · Rayagada, Odisha

    Paints village life, deities and nature in Saura's geometric language, and has exhibited at Dilli Haat and Pragati Maidan in New Delhi.

  • Nearly 30 years of practice

    Pappu Chand

    Miniature · Jaipur, Rajasthan

    Paints in the Mughal and Jaipur schools with pigments from flowers, vegetables and minerals, and has shown in Delhi, Ahmedabad, Pune and Jaipur.

  • Practising since 1984

    Ashok Sharma

    Miniature · Udaipur, Rajasthan

    Works on wasli paper and canvas with natural pigments, burnishing and gold and silver leaf, in the region of the Mewar school.

What co-design adds

When a traditional artist works with a designer, an architect or a contemporary artist, the skill stays the same but the scale, the format and the audience change. The result is work made for a hotel lobby, an office or an international exhibition, still made by hand by the artist.

Hotels, real estate developers, galleries and institutions can commission original or co-designed work through Tri-Impact's partner platforms, KalaAnchal and Dharohar Arts.

  • Sanjhi in Venice

    Mohan Kumar Verma's paper sculptures with artist Sumakshi Singh, cut from banana-fibre paper, were shown at Homo Faber 2026 in Venice.

  • Zardozi with Kalamkari

    Mohammad Bilal joins Zardozi embroidery with Kalamkari in shared wall pieces and carries a Mughal-era technique into contemporary work.

  • Paper-cut for new uses

    Ashutosh Verma has worked with Project Tarasha and Creative Dignity on new applications of Sanjhi paper-cutting.

How the initiative works

Living Art Traditions brings skills, provenance, buyers, savings and health together around each artist.

Living Art Traditions runs five supports together, around the same artists. Training raises the quality of the work, the record of origin carries the artist's name, the route to buyers turns the work into income, financial literacy helps that income last, and health care keeps the artist working. Each provides solutions to the challenges artists face.

Provenance

Every work carries a record of who made it, where and with what.

Every work in the initiative is made by hand, so no two are the same. Living Art Traditions keeps a record for each one: the artist, the tradition, the place, the materials and the colours. The record goes with the work to the buyer, through KalaAnchal and Dharohar Arts.

Questions

Questions from funders and buyers.

Which art forms does Living Art Traditions work with?

Madhubani, Gond, Bhil, Saura, Sohrai, Khovar, Tikuli, Warli, Phad, Sanjhi, Rajasthani and Pahari miniature, and Zardozi embroidery. The work includes paintings, paper-cuts and embroidery. The list grows as the initiative reaches new artists and states.

What does an artist receive through the initiative?

Training in quality, cost and time, co-design with designers and contemporary artists, financial literacy and savings, health check-ups and eye screening, a record of origin for each work, and a route to buyers through KalaAnchal and Dharohar Arts. The supports run together, around the same artists.

Can a company or foundation fund one part of the initiative?

Yes. CSR teams, foundations and institutions can fund the whole initiative or one part of it, such as training, health camps or financial literacy. A first conversation sets the artists, the places and the scope.

How does the initiative fit CSR priorities?

The initiative works on preventive health care, livelihood enhancement and the promotion of traditional arts, which are activities listed in Schedule VII of the Companies Act, 2013. Tri-Impact Global is registered with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs to undertake CSR activities (CSR00007814).

How can a hotel, developer or institution commission work?

Start a conversation with the space, the scale and the timeline in mind. Original and co-designed commissions are placed through KalaAnchal and Dharohar Arts, and each work carries its record of origin.

What does the record of origin contain?

The artist's name, the tradition, the place, the materials and method, and the colours used. It goes with the work to the buyer, so the artist is credited wherever the work is shown or sold.

How do KalaAnchal and Dharohar Arts work with the initiative?

KalaAnchal is an independent curatorial platform and Dharohar Arts is a partner platform. Tri-Impact works with artists on skills, finances, health and records of origin, and connects them to both platforms, which bring their work to institutions and buyers.

Support the artists behind the work.

Fund training, health and eye care or financial literacy for artists, or commission work through KalaAnchal and Dharohar Arts. A first conversation is the quickest way to see where Living Art Traditions fits your CSR or philanthropic priorities.
